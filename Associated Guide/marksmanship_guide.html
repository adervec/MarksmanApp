§ 01

How to Use This Guide

This guide is built for an Ontario sport shooter training at a 25-yard indoor range — Target Sports Canada in Gormley is used as the running example. It works whether you own your firearms or rent them at the lane, and whether you shoot a rimfire plinker, a service pistol, a revolver, or a carbine. Every drill, distance, and timing is designed for an indoor lane with the rules and rhythms of a public commercial range.

The structure follows how skill is actually built: fundamentals first, then a tour of what you can rent, then platform technique, then diagnostic tools to find what is breaking down, then a repeatable training cycle of dry-fire and live-fire. The 12-week plan in §11 ties it all together.

▸ Two Operating Principles

Dry-fire wins matches. Almost every measurable gain in trigger control, sight alignment, and recoil management comes from dry practice between range trips. Live ammo is for verification, not learning.

Slow is smooth, smooth is fast. Speed comes from eliminating wasted motion and bad reps, not from rushing. Build perfect shots first; speed up only when each shot is still perfect.

§ 02

The Seven Fundamentals

Every accurate shot — rifle or pistol, .22 or 9mm, bolt gun or revolver — is the product of the same seven elements. Most accuracy problems are a breakdown in one of these, and diagnosing which is the core skill of the sport.

01 / POSITION
Stance

Stable, repeatable, natural. The body is the platform; the firearm rests on it. A good position needs no muscle tension to hold the sights on target — that is natural point of aim.

02 / CONTACT
Grip / Hold

Firm and consistent contact between hand and firearm. Pistol: ~60% support hand, 40% firing hand. Rifle: firing hand is light; support and bone structure carry the weight.

03 / OPTICS
Sight Alignment

Front sight centred in the rear notch, equal light strips, tops level. This relationship is between the sights themselves — the target is secondary.

04 / AIM
Sight Picture

Aligned sights placed correctly on the target. 6 o'clock hold (front sight under the black) or centre hold, whichever your firearm is zeroed for.

05 / RHYTHM
Breath Control

Fire in the natural respiratory pause — after a normal exhale, before the next inhale. 4–7 second window. Do not hold until vision degrades.

06 / PRESS
Trigger Control

Smooth, straight-to-the-rear press that does not disturb sight alignment. The break should slightly surprise you — if you predict it, you are anticipating recoil.

07 / FINISH
Follow-Through

Hold the trigger to the rear, keep sights on target, call the shot before re-establishing. Releasing the trigger or moving the head before the bullet leaves destroys accuracy.

▸ The Most Important Habit

Call the shot. After every shot, before looking at the target, say where the sights were when the trigger broke. "Centre." "Low left." "High." If your call matches the hole, your fundamentals are working. If your call is good but the hole is elsewhere, the zero or ammo is off. If you cannot call the shot, you are not seeing the sights at the break — fix that first.

§ 03

Equipment & Ammunition

Eye and Ear Protection

Indoor pistol ranges are loud and full of muzzle blast. Double up: foam plugs underneath, over-ear muffs on top. Electronic muffs are worth the money — they cut the shot but pass range commands. Wraparound safety glasses are mandatory; yellow or light-amber lenses brighten the sight picture under typical indoor lighting.

Lead Exposure

▸ Indoor-Specific Risk

Indoor ranges concentrate airborne lead from primers and bullet bases. Reviews of Target Sports Canada flag ventilation as adequate but not exceptional. Wash hands and face with cold water and lead-removal soap (D-Lead, Hygenall) before eating, drinking, or touching your phone. Change clothes when you get home — especially if you have children. Wet-wipe your range bag interior. Annual blood-lead testing is reasonable for regular indoor shooters.

Ammunition — Rimfire (.22 LR)

If you shoot a .22 — your own bolt gun or a rental — rimfire is picky. Two rifles off the same line can prefer entirely different ammo. If you own the rifle, run a deliberate ammo test before settling on a load:

  1. Buy one box each of 4–6 different match or premium loads — CCI Standard Velocity, SK Standard Plus, RWS Target Rifle, Federal Gold Medal Match, Eley Sport, and Lapua Center-X if budget allows.
  2. Shoot five 5-shot groups with each, from a rest, at 25 yards, on a barrel that has been fouled with 5–10 rounds of that ammo first.
  3. Measure average group size (centre-to-centre of the two widest holes). Smallest = match ammo. Cheapest acceptable = practice ammo.

Avoid high-velocity hunting loads (Mini-Mag, Stinger) for precision — they group larger and shift POI between brands. On rentals you usually shoot the house ammo, which is fine for fundamentals.

Ammunition — Centrefire Pistol (9mm and friends)

Centrefire pistol calibres (9mm, .40 S&W, .45 ACP, .38 Special, .357 Magnum) are far less finicky than rimfire. A few universal notes:

  • Bullet weight: in 9mm, 115gr is cheapest and softest — ideal for fundamentals. 124gr is the common match weight. 147gr is heaviest, slowest, and pushes rather than snaps — easier on the wrist in long sessions.
  • Bullet profile: jacketed (FMJ) or plated. Avoid unjacketed lead in a revolver if your range bans it — verify before bringing reloads.
  • Brass vs. steel: some indoor ranges prohibit steel-cased ammo. Brass (Federal, Winchester, Blazer Brass, S&B) is universally accepted and cleaner.
  • Avoid +P for paper practice — it costs more and beats up the gun for no scoring benefit. Save it for any defensive-load familiarization.
  • Match the recoil to the goal: when first learning, the softest-shooting load you can find in the calibre builds better habits than a hot one.

If You Are Renting — Buy Range Ammo

Almost every range that rents firearms requires you to buy their ammunition for the rental (it protects their guns from unknown handloads). Budget for it. House ammo is typically clean, brass-cased FMJ in standard weights — perfectly good for everything in this guide.

Range Bag Checklist

  • Your firearm(s) cased, with trigger locks if travelling under ATT — or nothing to bring if you are renting
  • ATT and PAL/RPAL on your person — not in the case
  • Ammunition in original boxes or labelled containers
  • Eye protection (primary + spare); hearing protection (plugs + muffs)
  • Targets (B-8, NRA A-23, plain bull, sighting grid)
  • Stapler or pasters, masking tape, Sharpie, scorebook
  • Spotting scope or 8x–10x binoculars
  • Bore snake, patches, solvent, lubricant; hex keys / screwdriver bits for scope and action screws
  • Snap caps (.22 LR and 9mm) for dry-fire and malfunction practice
  • Lead-removal hand wipes, water, snack
§ 04

Rentable Platforms — What to Try

One of the best things about a range like Target Sports Canada — which keeps a stocked rental case and runs beginner packages — is that you can try a wide variety of firearms before owning anything. TSC's 20 pistol lanes accommodate pistols, carbines, and shotguns out to 25 metres. Rentals usually require buying the range's ammunition, and many ranges ask that first-timers shoot with an instructor or RSO. This is not an exhaustive catalogue — it is a diverse cross-section of what is popular and worth your time.

▸ How to Demo Smartly

Do not blow through ten guns in one visit — recoil fatigue and novelty wreck your fundamentals. Pick two or three per session that teach you something different (e.g. one rimfire pistol, one striker 9mm, one carbine). Shoot each slowly, on paper, and apply the same shot process from §02. The point of renting is to learn what fits your hand, your eyes, and the discipline you want to pursue.

▸ Renting vs. Owning

Renting needs no licence at most ranges that offer instructed shooting — it is a legal way to try the sport before committing. § 05

Rifle & Carbine Technique

Whether you are shooting a borrowed .22 bolt gun, a 10/22, or a rented carbine, the platform technique is the same: a stable position, a consistent cheek weld, and a clean trigger press that does not disturb the sights. The notes below cover bench and standing work at a 25-yard indoor lane.

Bench Position

  • Forend on a sandbag, bipod, or front rest — never on a hard surface. The barrel must not touch anything.
  • Rear bag under the toe of the stock. Elevation comes from squeezing or relaxing the rear bag, not from muscling the rifle.
  • Shoulder pocket: buttstock lives in the pocket formed by the deltoid and pectoral, loaded firmly enough that the rifle moves with you when you breathe.
  • Cheek weld: head comes down to the rifle, not the other way around. Same spot every time. On a rental, take a few dry seconds to find a repeatable weld before the first shot.
  • Firing hand: light. Pulls the rifle straight back into the shoulder, no torque or lift.
  • Support hand: pinches the rear bag for fine elevation, or rests still. Not gripping the rifle.
▸ Natural Point of Aim Test

Get into position with sights on target. Close eyes for 3 seconds, breathe normally, open them. If the sights have drifted, your body is not naturally aimed there — you are muscling. Slide the bag, adjust your hips, shift position until the rifle returns to target on its own. NPA is the single biggest force-multiplier in rifle shooting.

Working the Action

The action cycle is part of the shot, not a separate task. For a bolt gun:

  1. After firing, complete follow-through — eyes on sights, trigger held back, call the shot.
  2. Without removing your cheek, lift the bolt handle with thumb and index finger of the firing hand.
  3. Pull straight back, eject. Drive straight forward to chamber, rotate down to lock.
  4. Re-establish grip, settle, find sights, return to NPA. Only then begin the next shot.

For a semi-auto (10/22, PCC, carbine), there is no manual cycle — but the same discipline applies to recoil recovery: ride the recoil, let the sights settle back onto target, reset the trigger, and only then press again. Do not chase a fast cadence at the cost of the sight picture.

Optics & Zero

If the rifle wears a scope with adjustable parallax, set it to your shooting distance — 25 yards/metres indoors. Parallax error is the most overlooked cause of unexplained vertical stringing. For a red-dot on a carbine, both eyes open, put the dot where you want the hit; dot guns are fast and forgiving at indoor distances.

If you own the rifle, zero so POI is centre at 25 yards/metres with your chosen ammo. Rimfire has a flat trajectory out to 50 yards, so a 25-yard zero holds close enough for outdoor work.

§ 06

Pistol & Revolver Technique

This is the technique that carries across every handgun in the rental case — striker 9mm, hammer-fired DA/SA, 1911, or revolver. Grip and trigger control do 90% of the work; the rest is consistency.

Grip — The Most Important Section

A correct two-handed grip solves more problems than any other adjustment.

  1. Firing hand: the web of the hand drives high into the tang/backstrap. Ride it as high as possible — the higher the hand, the closer recoil runs through your forearm, and the flatter the gun shoots.
  2. Wrap fingers around the front of the grip. Trigger finger stays off the trigger until you are on target.
  3. Firing-hand thumb: high, pointed forward, relaxed. Do not curl it down — it pulls the muzzle off line.
  4. Support hand: meets the firing hand at ~45°. The heel fills the empty space on the grip panel left by the firing hand. Fingers wrap over the firing-hand fingers.
  5. Support-hand thumb: parallel to the firing-hand thumb, pointed forward, alongside the frame.
  6. Pressure: ~60% support, 40% firing. Both squeeze front-to-back, not side-to-side.
▸ Two Critical Safety Differences by Type

Semi-auto slide bite: keep both thumbs forward and low, clear of the reciprocating slide. A high firing-hand thumb behind the slide gets cut.

Revolver cylinder gap: a revolver vents flame and hot, lead-laden gas sideways from the cylinder gap on every shot. Keep both thumbs behind the front of the cylinder — never alongside or in front of the gap. This is the most common new-revolver-shooter injury.

Stance — Isosceles (Recommended)

  • Feet shoulder-width, toes pointed at the target.
  • Knees slightly bent, soft.
  • Hips square to the target.
  • Arms both extended, elbows straight but not locked. Forms an isosceles triangle from shoulders to hands.
  • Shoulders slightly rolled forward, weight on the balls of the feet.

Isosceles absorbs recoil evenly through both arms and is the easiest stance to repeat under any handgun. A bladed Weaver stance — firing-side foot back, support arm bent, push-pull tension between the hands — is the classic for one-handed bullseye precision (NRA Conventional Pistol) if you pursue that discipline.

§ 07

Sights, Trigger, Breathing in Detail

Sight Focus

Front sight sharp. Rear sight slightly blurred. Target distinctly blurred. The eye cannot focus on three planes simultaneously — if you focus on the target, the sights blur and small alignment errors translate into large misses.

  • Front post centred in the rear notch — equal light strips on both sides.
  • Top of front post level with top of rear notch — no see-saw.
  • The relationship between the sights is more important than where the sights are on the target. A perfectly aligned shot slightly off-centre is still a 9; a misaligned shot is anywhere.

Sight Picture Options

HoldDescriptionBest For
6 o'clockTop of front sight just under the bottom of the black bullseye. Sight "cradles" the bull.Bullseye / NRA Conventional
Centre holdTop of front sight in the centre of the bull, covering the X.Combat / silhouette / most defensive sights
Sub-sixFront sight a visible gap below the black.Bright lighting, small bulls, fine front blades

Pick one hold and zero the firearm for it. Switching between holds at different distances introduces error and shooter doubt.

Trigger — The Compressed Surprise Break

Press straight to the rear, smoothly, in a continuous motion, with rising pressure, until it breaks. You should know it is going to break, but not the exact instant. This eliminates anticipation — the flinch that pulls the shot low-left for a right-handed shooter.

  1. Index finger on the trigger with the pad — not the joint, not the tip.
  2. Take up any slack or pre-travel until you feel the wall.
  3. Increase pressure smoothly, straight to the rear, while sight alignment is held.
  4. The shot breaks.
  5. Trigger stays to the rear through recoil.
  6. Reset only after sights return to target.
▸ Ball-and-Dummy — The Diagnostic

Have a friend (or load the cylinder yourself, looking away) randomly mix live rounds and dummy rounds. When the gun fires, you cannot tell whether your flinch is yours or the recoil. When the hammer falls on a dummy, you see your flinch with absolute clarity — sights jump down and to the left as you "fight" a recoil that never came. There is no faster diagnostic for anticipation.

Breath Control

  1. Establish position, grip, sight picture.
  2. Take 2–3 normal breaths.
  3. Inhale, exhale partially (50–70%).
  4. Hold at the natural pause — no straining, no full lung.
  5. Press the trigger within 4–7 seconds of stopping.
  6. If you have not broken in 7 seconds, abort. Breathe again. Restart. Vision degrades past 7–8 seconds of breath hold.
§ 08

Diagnosing Your Groups

If shots land in a tight group somewhere other than centre, that is a zero or hold issue — fix with sight adjustment. If shots are scattered, that is technique. The location of the scatter tells you what is breaking down.

The classic shot-error chart for a right-handed shooter (mirror for left-handed):

Group Drifts ToLikely CauseFix
↙ LOW-LEFTAnticipating recoil (most common). Snatching trigger.Ball-and-dummy. Slow dry-fire. Lighten grip slightly.
↓ LOWBreaking wrist down at the break. Pushing the gun forward.Lock wrists. Stop pushing — let the gun recoil into you.
← LEFTToo much trigger finger (joint instead of pad). Thumbing the side.Re-check finger placement. Relax thumbs.
↘ LOW-RIGHTTightening grip during the press. Trigger plucking.Establish grip pressure before the press. Press only with the index finger.
→ RIGHTToo little trigger finger (tip only). Pushing trigger sideways.Move finger deeper. Press straight back.
↗ HIGH-RIGHTHeeling — pushing with heel of palm in anticipation.Relax firing hand. Let support hand carry grip pressure.
↑ HIGHJerking trigger. Head coming off rifle.Smooth press. Hold cheek weld through follow-through.
VERTICAL STR.Inconsistent shoulder pressure, breathing, parallax error.Same shoulder load. Same point in respiratory pause. Set parallax to range distance.

Use this chart only for groups that are otherwise tight in one direction. A 3-inch scatter with one flier means a single bad shot, not a systemic issue.

§ 09

Dry-Fire Program

Dry-fire is the highest-leverage practice you can do. It is free, requires no range trip, and isolates the fundamentals that recoil otherwise masks. Fifteen minutes a day of structured dry-fire will outpace one expensive live-fire session per week.

▸ Safety Protocol — Every Session

1. Choose a room with a safe direction — an exterior wall backed by something that will stop a round.
2. Remove all ammunition from the room. Put it behind a closed door.
3. Verify the firearm is unloaded — visually and physically. Magazine out, chamber open, look in, finger in. Three times.
4. State out loud: "This firearm is empty. I am beginning dry-fire practice."
5. When done: "Dry-fire is over." Do not reload in the same room.

Use snap caps for any firearm with a hammer or that should not be dry-fired on an empty chamber — most centrefire pistols and revolvers are fine, but rimfires must never be dry-fired without a snap cap, as the pin can peen the chamber edge. If you only shoot rentals, you can still dry-fire-practice the grip, stance, and trigger motion at home with an inert training pistol or a SIRT-style trainer.

Daily 15-Minute Routine

Block 1 — Stance & NPA (3 min)

  • Build your stance eyes-closed. Open eyes — sights on target? Adjust feet, not arms.
  • Repeat 5–10 times. Build the position automatically.

Block 2 — Pure Trigger Press (5 min)

  • Coin or empty casing balanced on the front sight.
  • Take aim, press the trigger. The coin should not fall.
  • Pistol: 10 single-action presses, then 10 double-action (DA is the harder skill — spend more time here).

Block 3 — Wall Drill (4 min)

  • Stand 3 feet from a blank wall. Aim at a single small mark.
  • Press the trigger while watching the front sight. It should not move when the hammer falls.
  • The simplest, most ruthless trigger-control diagnostic. Nowhere to hide.

Block 4 — Presentation / Bolt Cycle (3 min)

  • Pistol: low ready to sights on target, smoothly. A full-size steel pistol is heavier than a polymer one — practice with whatever you shoot most to build the muscle memory to hold it steady at extension.
  • Rifle: cycle the bolt without leaving cheek weld. Sights on target through the cycle. Five reps.

Weekly Variations

  • Once a week: dry-fire a full 10-shot bullseye string in real time. Call every shot.
  • Once a week: ball-and-dummy at the range with snap caps mixed in.
  • Once a week: weak-hand-only trigger press. Builds awareness of grip pressure.
§ 10

Live-Fire Drills

Every range session should have structure. Lane time is finite, ammunition is expensive, and unfocused shooting reinforces unfocused habits. Pick 2–3 drills per session, write down scores, compare to last week.

Targets

  • B-8 (NRA 25-yard pistol): the gold standard. 5.54" black, 8 ring ~8". Used in NRA Bullseye.
  • NRA A-23: the 25-yard smallbore rifle target.
  • Dot torture (free PDF): ten small circles, 50 rounds, structured multi-skill test.
  • Plain bull / grid: for ammo testing, zero confirmation, group measurement.

Rifle Drills — .22 or carbine, 25 yards

D1Five-Shot Group Builder

Standard precision drill. 5 shots, single aiming point, slow, full position and breath between each. Measure group size centre-to-centre of the two widest holes. Goal: under 1" at 25 yards with match ammo. Sub-half-inch is achievable with a good rifle, ammo, and shooter.

D2Cold-Bore Shot

First shot of the session, full deliberation, fresh aiming point. Mark where it lands. Track cold-bore shots over many sessions — for matches and hunting, the first shot from a cold, clean barrel is often the most important one.

D3NRA Smallbore 25-Yard Match Simulation

Two sighters, then 10 shots for record at an A-23. Score by ring value. Possible: 100. Solid sport shooter: 90+. Competitive: 95+ at 25 yards rested.

D4Standing Position

Once bench groups are under 1", stand up. Standing unsupported with a .22 bolt gun is humbling. At 25 yards, scoring 80+ on the A-23 standing is good. Olympic 10m air rifle and 50m smallbore are built on this position.

Pistol Drills — any handgun, 7 to 25 yards

D5Bill Drill — Modified for Revolver

Classic IDPA / USPSA drill: six shots into the A-zone (or 8-ring) at 7 yards, as fast as you can hit. Adapted for a six-shot revolver — a full cylinder. Start slow, every shot in the 8-ring. Build to a par of 4 seconds for six A-zone hits.

D6Slow Fire Bullseye (one-handed)

NRA Conventional Pistol slow-fire string: 10 shots in 10 minutes at a B-8, one-handed, single-action. At 25 yards with a service pistol or revolver, scoring 80 (eights and better) is a real benchmark; 90 is excellent.

D7Two-Handed Precision at 25 Yards

10 shots slow fire, two-handed, single-action, B-8. Target: group inside the 9 ring (under 6"). Most pistols are mechanically capable of much tighter — the shooter caps out before the gun does.

D8Double-Action Roll Drill

Cylinder loaded. All six shots double-action, deliberate, smooth. No cocking. 7 yards. Goal: all six inside the 8-ring. The hardest pure-trigger drill in the book. Trigger discipline must remain intact across all 12 lb of squeeze.

D9Strong-Hand-Only / Weak-Hand-Only

5 shots each at 7 yards on a B-8. Exposes weakness in grip-only stability. Most shooters lose 20–30 points dropping to one hand; the goal is to shrink that gap.

D10Dot Torture

50 rounds at 3–5 yards. A close indoor drill that works with any handgun. Different exercises on each of 10 dots. The classic "can you put 50 for 50?" benchmark. Start at 3 yards; move back as you clean it.

75-Minute Session Template

TimeActivity
00–10Set up. Eye/ear pro. Confirm targets. Verify zero with 5 slow shots. Log cold-bore separately.
10–25Fundamentals — one drill, focused. E.g. 25 deliberate shots on a B-8, slow fire, calling every shot.

§ 11

The 12-Week Plan

Assumes one range visit per week and 15 minutes of dry-fire 4–5 days per week. Total round count is roughly 600 .22 LR and 600 9mm over the cycle — modest, intentional, trackable.

01
Baseline
Range: Zero both firearms. Record cold-bore. 5-shot groups at 25 yd, both guns. Bullseye string for score. Dry: Stance and NPA. Coin drill. Wall drill.
02
Rifle fundamentals
Range: Five 5-shot groups, .22, bench. Measure all. Plus 10 standing shots if time. Dry: Bolt cycle without lifting head. Trigger press.
03
Pistol grip
Range: Grip diagnostic — 25 shots, two-handed, 7 yd, focus on 60/40 pressure. Dry: Grip-only with an empty/inert pistol. Build it 20 times per session.
04
Trigger control
Range: Ball-and-dummy with your pistol. Then 10-shot B-8 string at 15 yd two-handed. Dry: Wall drill, pistol. 50 perfect dry reps.
05
Sights
Range: Dry-fire warmup. 5-shot rifle group. 10-shot pistol B-8 at 25 yd two-handed. Dry: Front-sight focus on small target across the room.
06
Mid-cycle test
Range: NRA Smallbore A-23 simulation. NRA Bullseye slow-fire pistol string at 25 yd. Record scores. Dry: Short sessions. Reflect on weakest fundamental.
07
Revolver DA
Range: Double-Action Roll Drill. 30 rounds DA at 7 yd. Then 10 SA shots. Dry: DA dry-fire only. 50 perfect DA presses per day.
08
One-handed
Range: Bullseye one-handed at 25 yd, both firearms. Be honest — expect lower scores. Dry: One-handed coin drill. One-handed wall drill.
09
Standing rifle
Range: Standing rifle, .22, 25 yd. 20 shots scored on A-23. Compare to bench.
10
Speed under control
Range: Bill Drill modified — six shots, 7 yd. Slow first; build to par 5 sec. Dry: Presentation reps from low ready.
11
Dot Torture
Range: 50-round Dot Torture at 3 yd, then 5 yd, with your pistol. Dry: Maintenance — keep the streak.
12
Final test
Range: Re-run Week 1 protocol exactly. Score, measure, compare. Identify top 2 weaknesses for the next cycle. Dry: Light — taper like a sport athlete before a meet.

After 12 weeks, do not start over. Identify the two weakest skills from the post-test and design a new 8–12 week block around them. Skill development is cyclic, not linear.

§ 12

Range Day & Etiquette

Target Sports Canada is a 25-yard indoor commercial range in Gormley, north of Toronto, with lane-based shooting and a rental case. These notes apply there and at most indoor ranges — adjust to your venue's posted rules:

Before You Leave Home

If you are renting: bring photo ID and check the range's age, ID, and "must shoot with a companion" policies in advance. No licence is needed to shoot rentals under range supervision at most venues. That is the whole list.

If you own and are bringing your own:

  • Confirm your ATT covers the destination range (it must be listed).
  • Cased and locked for transport. Trigger lock on restricted firearms in addition to the case lock.
  • Ammunition in original packaging or labelled.
  • PAL/RPAL physically on you, not in the gun case.
  • Direct route from home → range → home. The ATT restricts you to that route.

Booking & Timing

  • Weekends and weekday evenings are busiest. Off-peak (Tuesday or Wednesday morning) gives a quieter range and more deliberate shooting. Beginner/rental packages and lanes book up fast — reserve ahead.
  • Plan minimum 90 minutes door-to-door. Less than an hour at the lane is hard to use well.
  • Booking blocks are typically 1 hour — book a double if you can, especially for rifle work that requires longer pacing between shots.

At the Lane

  • Uncase only at the firing line, muzzle downrange. Hard range rule and a sensible habit.
  • Lane lighting is uneven. Bring high-contrast targets if the printed ones wash out.
  • Test the target carrier before unloading your bag. Some have a reputation for sticking or running slow.
  • Revolver shooters: never let anyone stand close to your support side — the cylinder gap vents gas sideways. Semi-auto shooters: mind your ejection arc so you are not pelting your neighbour with hot brass.
  • Police your brass — even revolver casings — and keep your bay tidy.

Afterward

  • Lead-removal hand wipes before phone, keys, or anything you take home.
  • Range bag in the trunk, not the seat. Change shoes when you get home if you can.
  • If you own the firearm, clean it within 24 hours. Revolver cylinders and forcing cones collect carbon quickly; rimfire chambers and bolt faces accumulate wax and lead; semi-autos like a wipe-down and light lube on the rails.

§ 14

Mental Game

Once fundamentals are reliable, scoring is decided in the head. Bullseye shooters say it is 90% mental at the top level. These skills are practiced, not innate.

The Shot Process as a Routine

Every shot follows the same internal sequence. Build it in dry-fire until it runs without conscious effort:

  1. Settle into stance. Breathe.
  2. Grip and mount.
  3. Find natural point of aim.
  4. Sight focus — front sight sharp.
  5. Begin trigger press.
  6. Break. Follow through. Call the shot.
  7. Reset.

A bad shot interrupts the routine — abort and restart. A great shot completes it — trust it and reset. The routine is the same either way. Outcomes do not change the process.

Dealing with a Bad Shot

  1. Finish follow-through anyway.
  2. Make no immediate change — no equipment fiddling, no stance adjustment, no "try harder" muscle tension.
  3. Acknowledge the call, set down the firearm if needed, take three breaths, restart the routine.

Bad shots compound when shooters chase them; good shooters move on.

Pressure

If you intend to compete (Conventional Pistol, IPSC, smallbore matches at Ontario clubs), match performance is reliably worse than practice performance for the first several matches. This is normal.

  • Make practice harder than match conditions — tighter par times, lower allowed scores, more pressure on every drill.
  • Shoot the conditions, not the score. Stay in the routine. The scoresheet is read at the end, not during.
  • Eat, hydrate, sleep. Match-day fundamentals start the night before.
§ 15

Ontario Legal Refresher

▸ Not Legal Advice

This is a working refresher, not a substitute for the Canadian Firearms Safety Course (CFSC) or Canadian Restricted Firearms Safety Course (CRFSC), and not legal advice. The federal Firearms Act, the Criminal Code, and the conditions of your specific licence and ATT govern your conduct. When in doubt, the Chief Firearms Officer's office is the right call.

Licensing

  • A typical non-restricted firearm (most rifles and shotguns, e.g. a .22 bolt-action or a hunting rifle) requires a Possession and Acquisition Licence (PAL).
  • A restricted firearm (handguns and some rifles) requires a Restricted PAL (RPAL) and registration.
  • PAL and RPAL must be valid and unexpired. Carry them any time you are transporting or using firearms.

Transport

  • Non-restricted: unloaded, cased recommended, ammunition separate is best practice. Trigger lock is best practice though not strictly required for non-restricted in transit.
  • Restricted (handguns): unloaded, with a trigger lock, in a locked, opaque, sturdy case, with a valid ATT that lists the destination range.
  • ATTs are issued by the provincial CFO. In Ontario, an ATT is typically attached to the RPAL for transport to approved ranges and gunsmiths.
  • Direct route only. No unnecessary stops between home and the range.

Storage at Home

  • Non-restricted: unloaded, with a trigger or cable lock OR in a locked container, OR with the bolt removed. Ammunition with the firearm is permitted but separate is best practice.
  • Restricted: unloaded, trigger or cable lock, AND in a locked container (typically a safe). Ammunition separate is best practice.
  • Both: out of reach of anyone without lawful access.

The Four Universal Rules

  1. Treat every firearm as if it is loaded. Always.
  2. Never point the muzzle at anything you are not willing to destroy.
  3. Keep your finger off the trigger until your sights are on target and you have decided to fire.
  4. Be sure of your target and what is beyond it.

Range rules add a fifth in practice: only handle firearms at the firing line, muzzle downrange, when the range is hot.

§ 16

Ranges Near Toronto & Going Further

Disciplines Worth Exploring

  • NRA Conventional Pistol (Bullseye): one-handed slow, timed, and rapid fire on B-6/B-8 at 25 and 50 yards. Built for revolvers and target pistols. Slow, precise, mentally demanding.
  • IDPA / IPSC / USPSA: practical pistol competition — movement, draw, multiple targets, on the clock. Divisions exist for striker pistols, single-action, and revolvers. Many Ontario clubs run weekly or monthly matches.
  • ICORE: revolver-specific practical matches, if the wheelgun is your thing.
  • Smallbore Rifle (3-position or prone): the Olympic discipline. Iron sights, .22 LR, at 25 or 50 metres. A target rifle needs a peep sight and sling to compete formally, but the fundamentals are identical to anything you learn here.
  • Air rifle / air pistol (10 m): the other Olympic disciplines — and the cheapest, quietest way to shoot almost daily, even at home with a pellet trap.
  • PRS Rimfire: Precision Rifle Series rimfire matches are growing in Ontario. A bolt-action .22 at varied distances and positions fits the entry level perfectly.
  • Trap / Skeet / Sporting Clays: shotgun clay sports — a completely different, highly social discipline if you want to shoot moving targets outdoors.
  • F-Class / full-bore rifle: long-range precision out to 300–900 m at outdoor clubs, once you have the fundamentals and a centrefire rifle.
